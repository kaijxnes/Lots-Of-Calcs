How the comparison works

Comparing rent with a mortgage payment on its own misleads in both directions: part of each mortgage payment is building equity rather than being spent, but buying also ties up a deposit, costs thousands to get in and out of, and comes with repairs a landlord would otherwise pay for.

So the calculator follows two people from the same starting point. The buyer puts down the deposit and pays the buying costs; the renter invests that same money instead. Each month, whichever of them has the lower housing costs invests the difference. At the end, the buyer sells (paying selling costs) and both count what they have.

The example: a £300,000 home with a 10% deposit and a 4.5% mortgage over 25 years costs £1,501 a month in repayments, against £1,200 a month in rent. With prices and rents rising 3% a year and savings earning 5%, after 10 years the buyer is worth £198,934 and the renter £126,395 — buying by £72,539, with buying pulling ahead in year 3.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to rent or buy?

It depends mostly on how long you stay, how fast prices and rents rise, and what your savings could earn instead. With the example figures on this page — a £300,000 home, 10% deposit and £1,200 a month rent — after 10 years you'd be better off buying by £72,539, but after 2 years better off renting by £243: the costs of buying and selling take a few years to earn back.

What is the break-even point?

The first year in which, if you sold, you'd be better off for having bought than you would be from renting and investing the difference. Before it, the costs of buying and selling outweigh the equity you've built; after it, buying is ahead. The calculator shows it for your figures.

What does the comparison include?

For buying: the deposit, buying costs such as stamp duty and legal fees, mortgage payments, running costs (maintenance, insurance and service charges, as a percentage of the home's value) and the cost of selling at the end. For renting: the rent, rising each year. Whichever is cheaper each month has the difference invested, and the renter invests the deposit and buying costs from day one.

Why does the investment return matter so much?

Because the money a renter doesn't put into a deposit can grow elsewhere. If it could earn more than the home's price rises after costs, renting looks better; if it would sit in a low-interest account, buying usually wins. Try the example with 2% and 7% returns to see how much it moves.

What if house prices don't rise?

Then the buyer relies only on paying down the mortgage. With 0% price growth and the other example figures, after 10 years it's renting by £22,853. The table above shows the result at different growth rates.

Does it include stamp duty?

Put stamp duty and fees in the buying costs box. Stamp duty depends on the price, where you buy and whether you're a first-time buyer, and the rates change, so check the current rates on GOV.UK (or Revenue Scotland and the Welsh Revenue Authority) for your purchase.
