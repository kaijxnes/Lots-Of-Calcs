The three percentage formulas

Each tab answers one of the three questions people most often ask about percentages.

X% of Y — multiply Y by X and divide by 100. 15% of 80 is 80 × 15 ÷ 100 = 12.

X is what % of Y — divide X by Y and multiply by 100. 18 out of 24 is 18 ÷ 24 × 100 = 75%.

Percentage change — take the old value from the new one, divide by the old value and multiply by 100. A price going from £40 to £50 is (50 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = a 25% increase. Going back from £50 to £40 is (40 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = a 20% decrease — the same £10, but measured against a bigger starting figure.

Adding or taking off a percentage

To increase a number by a percentage, multiply it by 1 plus the percentage as a decimal: £60 plus 20% is 60 × 1.2 = £72. To decrease it, multiply by 1 minus the percentage: £60 less 15% is 60 × 0.85 = £51.

Working backwards is where people go wrong. If £72 already includes a 20% increase, the original is 72 ÷ 1.2 = £60 — not 72 less 20%, which gives £57.60. The same applies to removing VAT from a price; the VAT calculator does it for you.

Frequently asked questions

How do I work out what percentage one number is of another?

Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. For example 30 out of 120 is 30 divided by 120, times 100 — which is 25%.

How do I calculate a percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value and multiply by 100. Rent going from £900 to £990 a month is a 90 ÷ 900 × 100 = 10% increase. A negative answer means a decrease.

How do I find the original number before a percentage was added?

Divide by 1 plus the percentage as a decimal. If a price of £84 includes a 5% rise, the original was 84 ÷ 1.05 = £80. Taking 5% off £84 gives £79.80, which is wrong, because the 5% was worked out on the smaller original figure.

What is the difference between a percentage and a percentage point?

A percentage point is the plain difference between two percentages; a percentage change is that difference relative to where it started. An interest rate rising from 4% to 5% has gone up by one percentage point, but by 25% in relative terms.

Why is a 50% rise followed by a 50% fall not back where it started?

Because each percentage applies to a different base. 100 rising 50% is 150, and 50% of 150 is 75 — so you end at 75, not 100. Percentage falls are always taken from the larger, already-increased figure.

Is X% of Y the same as Y% of X?

Yes, always. 8% of 50 and 50% of 8 are both 4, because both are 8 × 50 ÷ 100. It is a handy shortcut when one way round is easier to do in your head.
