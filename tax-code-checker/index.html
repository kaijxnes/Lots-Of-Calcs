    A tax code tells your employer or pension provider how much tax-free income to give you from that job or pension. The numbers, times 10, are the tax-free amount; the letters describe your situation. You get a separate code for each job or pension, so a second job is often on BR, with your allowance used on your main one.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the most common UK tax code?

    1257L, for people with one job or pension and no untaxed income, unpaid tax or taxable benefits. It means you can earn £12,570 tax-free in the tax year.

    What do the numbers in my tax code mean?

    Multiply them by 10 for the income you can earn tax-free from that job or pension: 1257L means £12,570, 1100L means £11,000. A K code works the other way: K475 adds £4,750 to your taxable pay.

    What do the letters in my tax code mean?

    L is the standard allowance; M and N mean you've received or transferred Marriage Allowance; T means other calculations; K means untaxed income bigger than your allowance; BR, D0 and D1 tax everything at one rate (usually a second job); 0T gives no allowance; NT means no tax. S at the start means Scottish rates and C means Welsh rates.

    How do I know if I'm on an emergency tax code?

    If your code ends in W1, M1 or X (or your payslip shows NONCUM). 1257L on its own isn't an emergency code.

    What should I do if my tax code is wrong?

    Check and update your details (jobs, benefits, expenses, estimated income) with HMRC online or in the HMRC app. HMRC then sends you and your employer a new code within 15 working days, and any tax you've overpaid is usually refunded through your pay.
