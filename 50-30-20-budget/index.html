How the 50/30/20 rule works

The 50/30/20 rule splits what you take home, after tax, into three:

50% for needs — what you'd have to pay whatever happened: rent or mortgage, council tax, energy and water, food shopping, insurance, getting to work, minimum debt repayments and childcare.

30% for wants — everything you choose: eating out, takeaways, holidays, subscriptions, clothes beyond the basics, hobbies and nights out.

20% for savings — building an emergency fund first, then saving for goals, paying off debt faster than the minimum, and investing or pension top-ups.

It's a starting point, not a rule. Where rent is high, needs often take 60–70%, so 70/20/10 or 60/20/20 is more realistic; on a higher income, 40/30/30 saves more. The buttons above switch between them.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 50/30/20 rule?

A simple budget: half of your take-home pay goes on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on savings and paying off debt. It works on pay after tax, not your salary.

How much is 50/30/20 on £30,000?

£30,000 a year is about £2,093.30 a month after tax and National Insurance in England (2026/27). That gives £1,046.65 for needs, £627.99 for wants and £418.66 for savings.

Does rent count as a need?

Yes, along with council tax, utilities, food shopping, insurance, transport to work and minimum debt payments. If your needs come to more than half your pay, try 60/20/20 or 70/20/10 and look at the biggest bills first.

What should the 20% go towards?

An emergency fund of three to six months of needs first, then any expensive debt, then longer-term saving such as a pension or ISA. The emergency fund calculator works out the target and how long it takes.

Is 50/30/20 based on gross or net pay?

Net — what actually reaches your bank account. Pension contributions taken from your pay can count towards the 20%.
