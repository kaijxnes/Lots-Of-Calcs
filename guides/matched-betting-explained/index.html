Matched betting explained

How a back bet and a lay bet cancel out, how to size the lay stake, and what a free bet is really worth, worked through with real numbers.

The idea

Matched betting uses a bookmaker's free bet offers. You place a bet for an outcome at the bookmaker (a back bet) and a bet against the same outcome at a betting exchange (a lay bet), with stakes sized so that you end up with about the same amount whichever way the result goes. The two bets together cost a little, the qualifying loss; the free bet they unlock is worth much more.

A lay bet works the other way round from a normal bet: you're acting as the bookmaker. If the outcome loses, you win the lay stake (less the exchange's commission). If it wins, you pay the backer's winnings, the lay stake × (lay odds − 1): that's your liability, and the exchange holds it from your balance until the event is settled.

Step 1: the qualifying bet

To unlock a typical "bet £10, get a £10 free bet" offer you place £10 of your own money. The lay stake that balances it is:

lay stake = back odds × back stake ÷ (lay odds − commission)

Back £10 at 3.0 and lay at 3.1 with 2% commission: lay stake = 3.0 × £10 ÷ (3.1 − 0.02) = £9.74, with a liability of £20.45.

Either way you're down about £0.45: the qualifying loss. It comes from the gap between the back and lay odds, and the commission. The closer the two prices, the smaller it is. The lay bet calculator works out the stake for any odds and commission.

Step 2: the free bet

Most free bets are stake not returned (SNR): if the bet wins you get the winnings but not the £10 stake. So the lay stake is based on the winnings alone:

lay stake = (back odds − 1) × free bet ÷ (lay odds − commission)

A £10 free bet at 6.0, laid at 6.2 with 2% commission: lay stake = 5 × £10 ÷ 6.18 = £8.09. If the outcome wins, the bookmaker pays £50.00 and you pay the exchange £42.07: £7.93. If it loses, you keep the lay stake less commission: £7.93. About £7.93 either way, 79.3% of the free bet, against a £0.45 qualifying loss.

Why free bets go on higher odds

With a stake not returned free bet, the share you keep rises with the odds, because the missing stake matters less the bigger the winnings are. A £10 free bet with 2% commission, at typical lay prices a little above the back odds:

Higher odds also mean a bigger liability, so you need more money in your exchange account. Qualifying bets are the other way round: closer odds matter more than high ones, since you want the loss to be small. The odds converter turns fractional odds into the decimal odds these formulas use.

The risks and the catches

Mistakes cost real money. Laying the wrong outcome, the wrong market, or at odds that moved before your lay was matched leaves you exposed. Check every bet before placing it, and place the lay as soon as the back bet is on.

Offers have terms. Minimum odds, which bets count, and when the free bet expires all vary. Bookmakers can also limit the stakes or offers available to accounts that only take promotions.

It's still gambling. The bets are real, and you need enough money in the exchange to cover liabilities. If it stops feeling like arithmetic, stop. You must be 18 or over; free, confidential help is at GambleAware.

Frequently asked questions

What is matched betting?

Using bookmakers' free bet offers by backing an outcome at the bookmaker and laying the same outcome at a betting exchange, with stakes sized so the result doesn't matter. The small loss on the qualifying bet unlocks a free bet worth more.

How do I work out the lay stake?

For a normal bet: back odds × back stake ÷ (lay odds − commission). For a stake not returned free bet: (back odds − 1) × free bet ÷ (lay odds − commission). £10 at 3.0 laid at 3.1 with 2% commission needs a £9.74 lay.

What is lay liability?

What you pay out at the exchange if the outcome you laid wins: lay stake × (lay odds − 1). The exchange holds it from your balance until the event is settled.

How much is a £10 free bet worth?

Usually about £7 to £8 once it's laid off. A £10 stake not returned free bet at 6.0, laid at 6.2 with 2% commission, leaves £7.93 whichever way the result goes.

Why is there a qualifying loss?

The lay odds at the exchange are almost always a little higher than the back odds, and the exchange charges commission on winnings. Together they make the matched bet cost a little.
