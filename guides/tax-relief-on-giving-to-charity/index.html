Tax relief on giving to charity

How Gift Aid works, what you can claim back and how giving can lower the tax on the rest of your income.

How Gift Aid works

Donating through Gift Aid means charities and community amateur sports clubs (CASCs) can claim an extra 25p for every £1 you give. It will not cost you any extra. The charity claims back the basic rate tax you've paid on the gift, so £1,000 you give is worth £1,250 to them. You need to make a Gift Aid declaration for the charity, which can cover current and future donations and all donations made in the last 4 years.

Your donations will qualify as long as they’re not more than 4 times what you have paid in tax in that tax year (6 April to 5 April). If the charity or CASC gets back more tax than you’ve paid, HMRC may ask you to pay more tax to cover the difference. The Gift Aid calculator checks your donation against this.

Claiming back higher rate tax

You donate £100 to charity - they claim Gift Aid to make your donation £125. You pay 40% tax so you can personally claim back £25.00 (£125 x 20%). You can claim back the difference between the tax you’ve paid on the donation and what the charity got back when you fill in your Self Assessment tax return. It’s the same if you live in Scotland. You can claim through your Self Assessment tax return or by contacting HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC) and asking them to amend your tax code.

On an income of £60,000, a £1,000 donation lets you claim back £250.00, so it costs you £750.00; in Scotland, where that income is taxed at 42%, it's £275.00. On £35,000, a basic rate taxpayer has nothing extra to claim: the charity gets the relief (2026/27).

Gift Aid and your adjusted net income

If you made a Gift Aid donation, take off the ‘grossed-up’ amount — what you paid plus the basic rate of tax. So, for every £1 of Gift Aid donations you made, take £1.25 from your net income. That can matter more than the higher rate relief. You lose £1 of Personal Allowance for every £2 of adjusted net income over £100,000, so a donation that brings it back down restores allowance as well: on £110,000, giving £8,000 (£10,000 grossed up) gives you £4,000.00 back, and the gift costs you £4,000.00.

The High Income Child Benefit Charge is also worked out on adjusted net income: 1% of the Child Benefit for every £200 over £60,000, all of it at £80,000. A Gift Aid donation brings your income down by its grossed-up amount, the same as a personal pension contribution. The Child Benefit charge calculator shows what that saves.

Giving straight from your pay

If your employer or pension provider runs a Payroll Giving scheme, you get the tax relief as you give. To donate £1, you pay: 80p if you’re a basic rate taxpayer 60p if you’re a higher rate taxpayer 55p if you’re an additional rate taxpayer.

Leaving money to charity

Inheritance Tax is charged at 40% on the part of an estate above the threshold, or 36% if 10% or more of the estate goes to charity. The Inheritance Tax calculator works out the tax on an estate.

Frequently asked questions

How much does Gift Aid add?

25p for every £1 you give, so £100 becomes £125 for the charity at no extra cost to you.

Can higher rate taxpayers claim Gift Aid back?

Yes. You claim back the difference between the higher rate and the basic rate on the grossed-up gift, through your Self Assessment tax return or by asking HMRC to change your tax code. A £1,000 gift on £60,000 gives you £250.00 back (2026/27).

Is there a limit on Gift Aid?

Your donations qualify as long as they're not more than 4 times the tax you paid in that tax year (6 April to 5 April). If the charity gets back more tax than you've paid, HMRC may ask you to pay the difference.

Does Gift Aid reduce my adjusted net income?

Yes, by the grossed-up amount: take £1.25 off for every £1 you give. That can restore Personal Allowance lost over £100,000 and reduce the High Income Child Benefit Charge.

Does Gift Aid work in Scotland?

Yes, and you claim higher rate relief the same way. The Scottish basic rate limit and every limit above it are extended by the grossed-up gift.
