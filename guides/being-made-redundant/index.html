Being made redundant: money and tax

What you're owed, what's tax-free and what comes off, from GOV.UK's rules.

Statutory redundancy pay

With at least 2 years' service, statutory redundancy pay is half a week's pay for each full year you were under 22, a week's pay for each full year from 22 to 40, and a week and a half for each full year from 41. Weekly pay is capped at £751 and service at 20 years, so the most is £22,530 (for redundancies from 6 April 2026). Your employer can pay more than this as enhanced redundancy or a severance payment. The redundancy pay calculator works out the statutory amount.

The £30,000 tax-free amount

You do not usually pay tax on the first combined £30,000 of: statutory redundancy pay additional severance or enhanced redundancy payments your employer gives you non-cash benefits, for example company property you keep after your employment ends. You’ll pay tax on any amount over a combined total of £30,000. That part pays Income Tax but not employee National Insurance.

Taxed as normal pay: You’ll pay tax and National Insurance on: unpaid wages holiday pay bonuses payments you get from your employer for agreeing to enter into a restrictive covenant any payments you receive instead of working during your notice period - this may be payment in lieu of notice (PILON) or be part of any severance pay. Not taxed at all: You will not pay tax and National Insurance on: contributions your employer makes to a registered pension scheme as part of your termination payment - you’ll pay tax on any employer contributions that go above the Annual Allowance legal costs related to the settlement that your employer pays directly to your solicitor a termination payment you get because of an injury, illness or disability that prevents you from being able to continue to do your job.

Notice you don't work

You’ll pay tax and National Insurance on the part of your termination payment equivalent to what you’d have earned if you were working. This may apply to: lump sum payments in lieu of notice (PILON) pay you’re given while on ‘gardening leave’ (where you remain on the payroll but you’re asked not to work) part of any severance, enhanced redundancy or non-cash benefits you get known as Post-Employment Notice Pay (PENP). If the amount of PENP is more than the total of any severance, enhanced redundancy or non-cash benefits you receive, you will only pay tax on the amount you actually get. Statutory redundancy pay is never Post-Employment Notice Pay. GOV.UK's example: £10,000 of statutory redundancy pay and £5,000 of severance, with 4 weeks' notice not worked at £500 a week, means tax and National Insurance on £2,000 of the severance, and the other £13,000 is tax-free.

A worked example

Say you earn £40,000 a year and leave in December with £15,000 of statutory redundancy pay, £25,000 of severance and £2,000 of holiday pay, having worked your notice. £30,000 is tax-free, the other £10,000 of redundancy and severance pays Income Tax only, and the £2,000 holiday pay pays tax and National Insurance like wages. Paid with your final pay, about £3,259.50 tax and £91.35 National Insurance come off, leaving £38,649.15 (2026/27, England, Wales and Northern Ireland). The redundancy tax calculator works out your own, including Scottish rates.

Paid after your P45

Any tax and National Insurance due on your termination payment will be taken automatically by your employer in your final payslip. If you get your termination payment after you have got your P45, your employer will use an ‘0T’ tax code. Tax will be deducted on the assumption that you have used up your personal allowance for the current tax year. So the same payment made after your P45 has about £4,250.25 tax taken off instead of £3,259.50. If you've no more income in the tax year, the tax due on it for the year is only about £2,400.00, so either way you'd have paid too much. If you think that you’ve paid too much tax or you think that your termination payment has not been taxed in the right way, contact HMRC.

If you've paid too much or too little tax by the end of the tax year (5 April), HMRC sends a tax calculation letter (P800) or a Simple Assessment letter. The tax refund calculator estimates what you might be owed, and the leaving a job guide covers notice and holiday pay.

Frequently asked questions

Is redundancy pay taxed?

The first £30,000 of statutory redundancy pay, severance and non-cash benefits combined is usually tax-free. Anything over that pays Income Tax but not employee National Insurance. Holiday pay, unpaid wages, bonuses and pay in lieu of notice are taxed as normal pay.

How much statutory redundancy pay will I get?

It depends on your age, weekly pay (capped at £751) and years of service (up to 20), so the most is £22,530. You need at least 2 years' service.

Is pay in lieu of notice tax-free?

No. Pay in lieu of notice, and the part of severance equal to the pay for notice you didn't work (Post-Employment Notice Pay), pays tax and National Insurance.

Why was so much tax taken off my redundancy pay?

If it's paid after you've got your P45, your employer uses the 0T tax code, which assumes you've used up your Personal Allowance. If you think you've paid too much tax, contact HMRC.

Do I pay National Insurance on redundancy pay?

Not on statutory redundancy pay or severance (other than notice pay), even above £30,000. You do on holiday pay, unpaid wages, bonuses and pay for notice.
