How the split works

Each person's share is their figure divided by everyone's figures added together. In % mode you type the shares you've agreed; they don't need to add up to 100, because they're scaled to fit. In income mode you type each person's income and the shares follow from it, so someone earning twice as much pays twice as much.

Worked example: a couple pay £1,850 a month in rent, council tax and bills. One takes home £2,700 a month and the other £1,800. Together that is £4,500, so their shares are 60% and 40%: £1,110 and £740. Each puts the same 41% of their take-home pay into the household, leaving £1,590 and £1,060.

Choosing a fair split

ApproachWorks well when
Equal sharesIncomes are similar, or for housemates who share the space equally
In proportion to incomePartners earn very different amounts and want the same share of their pay going on bills
Agreed percentagesRooms differ in size, someone works from home and uses more energy, or one person is away a lot

Use take-home pay rather than gross salary for the income split: it's what each person actually has to spend. The salary after tax calculator gives it for a gross salary. Whatever you choose, review it when someone's pay or circumstances change.

What to include

Typical shared costs are rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, water, broadband, TV licence, phone contracts if shared, and a food shop if you buy it together. Annual bills such as insurance or the TV licence are easiest to share monthly: divide the yearly cost by 12.

If one person lives alone they can claim a 25% council tax single person discount; when a household changes size, it's worth checking whether the council tax bill should change too.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fairest way to split household bills?

Splitting in proportion to income is the usual answer when housemates or partners earn very differently, since it keeps the burden even relative to what each person can afford. An even split is simpler when incomes are similar.

Do the percentages have to add up to 100?

No. Shares are normalised automatically, so entering 60 and 30 splits the bills two-thirds and one-third just as 66.7 and 33.3 would.

Should I split bills by gross or take-home pay?

Take-home pay is usually fairer, because tax takes a bigger share of higher salaries. On gross pay, the higher earner ends up paying a larger share of what they actually take home than the lower earner does.

How do housemates split bills when rooms are different sizes?

Agree a percentage for each person — for example 40% for the largest room with an en-suite and 30% each for the other two — and enter those in % mode. Many households split the rent by room and the utility bills equally.

How do we handle a bill that is paid once a year?

Enter it as a monthly amount by dividing the yearly cost by 12, so it is shared in the same proportions as everything else and nobody is caught out by a large bill.

Is a joint account better than paying each other back?

A joint account for bills, with each person paying in their share each month, avoids a running tally of who owes what. Use the amounts in the “Owes” column as each person's monthly transfer.
