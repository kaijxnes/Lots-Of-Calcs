Take-home pay by country, 2026

How much of the same salary you keep after income tax and social contributions, in 26 places.

The short version

On a €50,000 salary, Switzerland (Zug) leaves the most: 91.2%. Belgium leaves the least: 64.6%. That's a difference of €13,280 a year on the same pay. The typical place keeps about 76.9%.

The UK (England, Wales and Northern Ireland) ranks 5 of 26, keeping 80.4% of £41,868. Rankings shift with salary: the UK is 8 on €30,000 and 6 on €100,000.

How it's worked out

For each place: a single employee on the salary shown, with that place's default options, using the current tax year's income tax and employee social contributions (National Insurance, social security, health and unemployment insurance and similar), plus compulsory workplace pension contributions where they're taken from pay, as with Switzerland's occupational pension. Voluntary pension saving isn't included. Nor are costs that aren't taken from pay, which matter most for the places at the top: Switzerland's compulsory health insurance premiums and US health plan premiums are paid separately and would lower their take-home. Employer contributions, benefits and credits for children are left out too; each place's calculator lists what it doesn't model. The figures come from the same calculators as the rest of the site, whose tax files are checked against official sources every month; how we check our figures explains the process.

Frequently asked questions

Which country lets you keep the most of your salary?

Of the 26 places here, Switzerland (Zug): a €50,000 salary leaves CHF 42,043 (€45,585), or 91.2%, after income tax and employee social contributions. Belgium leaves the least, 64.6%.

How does the UK compare?

The UK (England, Wales and Northern Ireland) ranks 5 of 26 on a €50,000 salary (£41,868), keeping 80.4%. On €30,000 it ranks 8, and on €100,000, 6.

Why do the rankings change with salary?

Countries tax in different ways: some have a large tax-free allowance and steep top rates, others take a flat share in social contributions from the first euro, and some cap contributions. So a country can be cheap on a modest salary and expensive on a high one, or the other way round.

Does this account for the cost of living?

No. It compares only what's taken from the same gross salary. Prices, rents, healthcare and pensions differ a lot: in Switzerland, for example, health insurance is compulsory but paid separately, not from pay. What a given job pays also differs from country to country, so take-home pay is one part of the picture.
