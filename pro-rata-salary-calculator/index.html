How pro rata pay works

Part-time workers shouldn't be treated less favourably than comparable full-time workers just because they work fewer hours. That means the same hourly pay rate, and benefits such as bonuses and holiday in proportion to the hours: GOV.UK's example is a part-time worker on half the hours getting £500 of a £1,000 Christmas bonus. Overtime rates may only start once you've worked more than a full-time worker's normal hours. For holiday, the holiday entitlement calculator does the pro rata sum; for an hourly rate, the hourly to salary calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How do I work out a pro rata salary?

Divide your hours by the full-time hours and multiply by the full-time salary. On a 37.5-hour week, 22.5 hours (3 days) is 60%, so £30,000 pro rata is £18,000. GOV.UK's example: working half the hours of a full-time worker who gets a £1,000 bonus, a part-time worker should get £500.

What counts as full time?

There's no legal number of hours, but GOV.UK says a full-time worker will usually work 35 hours or more a week. Use your employer's full-time hours, often 37.5 or 40.

Will I pay less tax working part time?

Yes, as a share of pay. The Personal Allowance and National Insurance threshold don't shrink when you go part time, so more of a smaller salary is tax-free: 3 days a week on £30,000 keeps 65.6% of the full-time take-home for 60% of the hours.

Do part-time workers get the same holiday and benefits?

Yes, in proportion: part-time workers should get the same treatment for pay rates, pensions, holidays and training, with some benefits applied pro rata. The holiday entitlement calculator works out pro rata holiday.
