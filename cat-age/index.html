How it works

The conversion follows the standard feline lifestage method:

First year ≈ 15 human years
Second year adds ≈ 9 more, so a two-year-old cat is about 24
Every year after that adds about 4

The front-loading reflects reality. A one-year-old cat is fully grown, sexually mature and perfectly capable of having kittens of its own — nothing like a seven-year-old child. By two it has reached the feline equivalent of a young adult, and from then on the pace settles down to roughly four human years for each of its own.

Worked example

A seven-year-old cat: 15 for the first year, +9 for the second gives 24, then five more years at 4 each adds 20. That comes to 44 human years — comfortably middle-aged, and the point at which vets typically start recommending annual senior health checks and watching for kidney and thyroid changes. The old ×7 rule would have said 49.

Why "multiply by seven" is wrong

The seven-year rule is a piece of folklore, not science, and it goes wrong immediately. It would make a one-year-old cat the equivalent of a seven-year-old child — yet that cat is fully grown and can breed. No seven-year-old human is in that position. The real figure for the first year is around 15.

It then overshoots at the other end. Multiplying by seven puts a fifteen-year-old cat at 105, well past the human record, when the lifestage method gives a more sensible 76. Because the rule uses one flat multiplier, it cannot capture the thing that actually defines feline ageing: very fast early, much slower later.

These are averages across the population. Indoor cats generally outlive outdoor ones by a wide margin, and breed, weight and health all shift the picture, so use the number as a guide rather than a diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

Is one cat year seven human years?

No. It is a myth. A cat's first year is worth about 15 human years and the second about 9 more, after which each year adds roughly 4. A flat multiplier of seven misrepresents both the rapid start and the slower later years.

How old is a 1-year-old cat in human years?

About 15. At twelve months a cat is fully grown, has its adult teeth and can reproduce, which lines up far better with a teenager than with a small child.

When is a cat considered senior?

Around 11 years old, which is roughly 60 in human terms. Vets commonly treat 7 to 10 as mature, 11 to 14 as senior and 15 upwards as geriatric, with more frequent check-ups recommended from the mature stage onwards.

Does breed or size change a cat's age conversion?

Not materially, which is the big difference from dogs. Feline lifespans vary far less by size than canine ones, so the same conversion works for a Maine Coon and a Siamese. Lifestyle matters much more — indoor cats typically live considerably longer than outdoor ones.

How do I work out my kitten's age in human years?

Enter a decimal — 0.5 for six months, 0.25 for three months. The first year scales at about 15 human years, so a six-month-old kitten comes out at roughly 7 to 8.
