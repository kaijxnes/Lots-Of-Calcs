How holiday works for irregular hours

If your hours change from one pay period to the next, or you work only part of the year, you build up holiday as you work: 12.07% of the hours you work in each pay period, for leave years starting on or after 1 April 2024. GOV.UK rounds it to the nearest hour: under half an hour rounds down, even to nothing, and half an hour or more rounds up. GOV.UK's example is 30 hours in a week earning 4 hours of holiday (30 × 12.07 ÷ 100 = 3.621), which you can take from the next pay period. If you're paid weekly and work 4 hours a week or less, GOV.UK says it may be appropriate for your employer to round up to the next half hour or hour so you still build up holiday.

Why it's 12.07%

Almost all workers are entitled to 5.6 weeks' paid holiday a year, which leaves 46.4 weeks of work (52 minus 5.6). 5.6 ÷ 46.4 is 12.07%, so 12.07% of the hours you work over 46.4 weeks comes to 5.6 weeks of holiday. That's also the most statutory holiday you can build up in a year. If your contract gives more holiday, the percentage is the weeks of holiday ÷ the weeks left to work × 100: GOV.UK's example is 6 weeks giving 6 ÷ 46 = 13.04%. Rolled-up holiday pay

Instead of paying you when you take holiday, your employer can add holiday pay to every payslip: 12.07% of your total pay for the pay period, shown as a separate item and paid on top of your normal pay, which must be at least the minimum wage. It's only allowed for irregular-hours and part-year workers, and not if your leave year began on or before 31 March 2024. Your pay for the period includes overtime and shift premiums: GOV.UK's example is 20 hours at £11 and 20 hours at £12, £460 in total, giving £55.52 of holiday pay.

If your employer doesn't roll it up

You're paid when you take holiday. All of an irregular-hours or part-year worker's leave is paid at the "normal" rate, which includes commission, regular overtime and payments for length of service or qualifications, but not usually bonuses. A week's pay is your average pay over the previous 52 weeks, counting only weeks you were paid; your employer can count back up to 104 weeks to find them.

Who counts as irregular hours or part-year

You're an irregular-hours worker if your contract makes the paid hours you work in each pay period wholly or mostly variable, as on a zero-hours or casual contract. A fixed rota with no overtime, such as 15 hours one week and 20 the next, isn't irregular. You're a part-year worker if your contract has you work only part of the year, with periods of at least a week when you don't work and aren't paid, such as seasonal work, even on fixed hours. Everyone else gets 5.6 weeks a year worked out in days: the holiday entitlement calculator does that, and the pro rata calculator covers part-time pay.

Frequently asked questions

What is 12.07% holiday pay?

Irregular-hours and part-year workers build up holiday at 12.07% of the hours they work in each pay period. If their employer uses rolled-up holiday pay, they get 12.07% of their pay for the period on top, instead of being paid when they take the holiday.

How many hours of holiday do I get for 40 hours of work?

4.828 hours, which rounds to 5 hours. For 20 hours it's 2.414 hours, rounding to 2 hours.

Why is it 12.07% and not 12.5%?

You're entitled to 5.6 weeks of holiday out of 52, which leaves 46.4 weeks of work. Holiday builds up while you work, so it's 5.6 ÷ 46.4, which is 12.07%.

How is the holiday rounded?

To the nearest hour in each pay period: less than half an hour rounds down, even to zero, and half an hour or more rounds up. GOV.UK says that if you're paid weekly and work 4 hours a week or less, your employer may round up to the next half hour or hour.

Can my employer pay rolled-up holiday pay?

Only if you're an irregular-hours or part-year worker and your leave year started on or after 1 April 2024. It must be shown separately on each payslip and paid on top of your normal pay.

What if my contract gives more than 5.6 weeks?

Work out your own percentage: weeks of holiday ÷ the weeks left to work × 100. GOV.UK's example is 6 weeks: 6 ÷ 46 = 13.04%.
