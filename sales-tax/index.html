How it works

Adding tax is the easy direction. Multiply the price before tax by the rate and add it on:

tax = net × rate ÷ 100
gross = net + tax

Taking tax back off is where it goes wrong. The tax was worked out on the smaller number, so you cannot just subtract the same percentage from the bigger one. You have to divide:

net = gross ÷ (1 + rate ÷ 100)
tax = gross − net

Take £300 including 20% VAT. Knocking 20% off gives £240, and that is wrong — £240 plus 20% is £288, not £300. Dividing by 1.2 gives the right answer, £250, and £250 plus 20% is exactly £300.

There is a shortcut that UK accountants use constantly. The share of a tax-inclusive price that is tax comes to rate ÷ (100 + rate), which at 20% is 20/120, or one sixth. So the VAT in any 20% gross figure is just the total divided by six: £300 ÷ 6 = £50. The calculator shows this fraction whenever you are removing tax.

Worked example

You are quoting for a job at £250 before VAT, standard UK rate.

Adding: 250 × 0.20 = £50 tax, so you invoice £300.

Removing: a supplier sends you a receipt for £300 with no VAT breakdown. 300 ÷ 1.2 = £250 net, so the VAT was £50 — or straight to it, 300 ÷ 6 = £50.

The two directions are mirror images, which is a useful check: whatever you get one way should come back the other.

Sales tax, VAT and GST

Different names, meaningfully different systems, and the difference matters if you are trading across borders.

VAT (used across the UK, EU and much of the world) is charged at every stage of the supply chain, but businesses reclaim the VAT they paid on their own purchases. Only the end consumer really bears it. Advertised prices to consumers normally already include it.

GST — in Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, India and Canada — works the same way as VAT. It is essentially a naming difference, though the details vary.

US sales tax is a genuinely different animal. It is charged once, at the final retail sale, and there is no federal rate at all. States set their own, and cities, counties and transit districts stack more on top, which is why a single state can contain dozens of effective rates. American prices on the shelf are also normally quoted before tax, so the total at the till is higher than the label — the opposite of the UK convention.

That last point is why the US presets here are state base rates only. To get an exact figure for a specific address you need the state rate plus every local rate that applies to it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remove VAT from a price?

Divide by 1 plus the rate as a decimal — for 20% VAT, divide the gross price by 1.2. Do not subtract 20%, which gives a different and wrong answer: £300 minus 20% is £240, but the correct figure before VAT is £250. Quicker still at 20%, divide the total by 6 to get the VAT itself.

What is the VAT fraction?

The proportion of a VAT-inclusive price that is VAT, worked out as rate ÷ (100 + rate). At the UK standard rate of 20% that is 20/120, which simplifies to 1/6. At the 5% reduced rate it is 5/105, or 1/21. It is a handy shortcut for pulling the tax straight out of a gross figure without dividing first.

Why is US sales tax not a single national rate?

Because the United States has no federal sales tax. Each state sets its own, and local jurisdictions add theirs on top, so the rate depends on the exact address of the sale rather than the state alone. Five states — Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon — charge no state sales tax at all, though some Alaskan towns levy their own.

Is VAT the same as GST?

In substance, mostly yes. Both are charged at each stage of the supply chain with businesses reclaiming what they paid on their inputs, so the end consumer carries the cost. The name varies by country — VAT in the UK and EU, GST in Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, India and Canada, and consumption tax in Japan. US sales tax is the genuine odd one out, being charged only once at the final sale.

Are these rates kept up to date?

They were checked in August 2026, and the notable recent changes are reflected — India's move to 5%, 18% and 40% slabs in 2025, Nova Scotia's cut to 14%, Finland at 25.5% and Switzerland at 8.1%. Rates still change with budgets, and which one applies depends on precisely what you are selling, so treat these as a starting point rather than tax advice.

What is the difference between zero-rated and exempt?

Zero-rated sales are taxable, just at 0%, so the business can still reclaim the VAT on its own costs. Exempt sales are outside the system, and VAT on related costs generally cannot be reclaimed. Most food, children's clothes and books are zero-rated in the UK; insurance, postage and most financial services are exempt. The distinction changes nothing for the customer but a great deal for the seller.
