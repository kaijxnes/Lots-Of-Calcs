About this calculator

Dutching means backing more than one selection in the same market, splitting your stake so that the payout is identical no matter which of them comes in. The stake on each selection is proportional to its implied probability: total stake × (1 ÷ that selection's decimal odds) ÷ the sum of 1 ÷ odds across every selection.

The return is simply the total stake divided by that same sum. If the combined implied probability of your selections comes to less than 100%, the return beats your outlay and the profit is guaranteed — provided one of them wins. If it comes to more than 100%, which is usual once the bookmaker's margin is included, every outcome loses money and the calculator says so.

The combined implied probability figure is the key number to watch: it is the sum of 1 ÷ odds across your selections, expressed as a percentage. Below 100% is an arbitrage; above it, you are paying the bookmaker's overround.

Assumptions: exactly one selection can win, all bets are with the same bookmaker at the prices entered, and no commission is deducted. If you are dutching across an exchange, subtract commission from the returns yourself, and note that a dead heat will reduce the payout on the affected selection.

How to dutch a bet: worked example

You fancy three horses in a race at 2/1, 3/1 and 5/1 and want to bet £50 in total, winning the same whichever of them wins.

1. Convert to decimal and find each implied chance. 2/1 is 3.00 (33.3%), 3/1 is 4.00 (25%) and 5/1 is 6.00 (16.7%). Together that's 75%.

2. Split the stake in proportion. Each horse gets its share of the 75%: 33.3/75 of £50 is £22.22, 25/75 is £16.67 and 16.7/75 is £11.11.

3. Check the return. Every winner pays the same: £22.22 × 3 = £16.67 × 4 = £11.11 × 6 = £66.67, a £16.67 profit. If none of the three wins, all £50 is lost.

The shortcut: return = total stake ÷ combined implied probability, so £50 ÷ 0.75 = £66.67. The more selections you add, the higher the combined percentage and the smaller the profit, until at 100% you only get your money back.

Where dutching is used

Horse racing: backing two or three runners you think will beat the rest, as in the example. Football correct scores: covering several likely scores, such as 1-0, 2-0 and 2-1, for one return. Each-way extra places and price boosts: spreading a stake where one price is generous. In every case the maths is the same; only the number of selections changes.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate dutching stakes?

Turn each price into decimal odds, find 1 ÷ odds for each selection, and add them up. Each selection's stake is the total stake × (1 ÷ its odds) ÷ that sum. £50 across 2/1, 3/1 and 5/1 gives £22.22, £16.67 and £11.11, returning £66.67 whichever wins.

How many selections can I dutch?

As many as you like, but each one adds to the combined implied probability. Once that reaches 100% the return only matches your stake, and above it every winner loses money, so two to four selections is typical.

Is dutching the same as matched betting?

No. Dutching backs several outcomes at a bookmaker so that any of them pays the same; you still lose if none of them wins. Matched betting backs at a bookmaker and lays the same outcome on an exchange — see the lay bet calculator.

Is dutching guaranteed to make a profit?

Only when the combined implied probability of your selections is under 100%, which is uncommon at a single bookmaker because of their built-in margin. Otherwise dutching just spreads risk across several outcomes at a small expected cost.

How is the stake split across selections decided?

In proportion to each selection's implied probability. Shorter-priced selections get a larger share of the stake, which is exactly what makes every possible return come out the same.

What happens if I round the stakes?

The returns stop being perfectly equal. Rounding to the nearest pound is usually fine for small stakes, but the more you round, the more the payouts drift apart between outcomes.
