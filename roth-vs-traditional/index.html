How it works

This compares the same cost to your paycheck: a traditional contribution is made before tax, so it can be larger — $500 out of take-home at a 22% rate funds about $641 of traditional contributions. If you instead put the same dollar amount into each, the Roth holds more after-tax money. Employer matches go in pre-tax either way, so they don't change the comparison.

Limits for 2026

401(k), 403(b) and 457 plans: up to $24,500 of your own contributions in 2026, Roth and traditional combined, plus an $8,000 catch-up from age 50 ($11,250 at 60 to 63).

IRAs: up to $7,500 in 2026 across Roth and traditional IRAs, plus $1,100 from age 50.

Roth IRA contributions phase out between $153,000 and $168,000 of modified AGI for single filers ($242,000 to $252,000 married filing jointly) in 2026.

Sources: IRS — 401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026, IRA limit increases to $7,500; IRS Notice 2025-67 — 2026 retirement plan amounts. See also what monthly investing could grow to.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Roth or a traditional account better?

It comes down to your tax rate now against your tax rate when you take the money out. For the same cost to you, the traditional account wins if you'll pay less tax later than you save now; the Roth wins if you'll pay more. At 22% now the two break even at about 22.0% later. In the example, $500 a month for 30 years leaves $487,256 in the Roth and $549,725 from the traditional account after tax.

Why does the same monthly amount buy a bigger contribution?

Because traditional account contributions are made before tax. Paying $500 from take-home at a 22% rate funds $641 a month of contributions, so the traditional account grows to $624,688 — but $74,963 of that is tax when it comes out.

What are the limits?

401(k), 403(b) and 457 plans: up to $24,500 of your own contributions in 2026, Roth and traditional combined, plus an $8,000 catch-up from age 50 ($11,250 at 60 to 63). IRAs: up to $7,500 in 2026 across Roth and traditional IRAs, plus $1,100 from age 50. Roth IRA contributions phase out between $153,000 and $168,000 of modified AGI for single filers ($242,000 to $252,000 married filing jointly) in 2026.

Should I just use both?

Many people do: the traditional account for money taxed at a high rate now, the Roth for flexibility, since Roth contributions (not earnings) can be taken out at any time. If you aren't sure what your tax rate will be later, splitting between them hedges the guess.
